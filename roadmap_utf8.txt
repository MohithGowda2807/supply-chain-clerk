1. Problem Statement
1.1 Industry Context
Pharmaceutical and herbal product warehouses operate under strict regulatory mandates that require complete batch-level traceability ù from the moment a shipment arrives at the receiving dock to the point it leaves as a dispensed or shipped order. The inta
ke process is the foundational step of this chain. When it fails, every downstream process ù inventory accuracy, expiry management, recall readiness, and compliance reporting ù is compromised.
Despite decades of supply chain digitisation, the physical inta
ke desk at small-to-mid-size pharmaceutical and herbal warehouses remains stubbornly manual. Workers receive physical packing slips, handwritten invoices, and product labels in dozens of formats from hundreds of suppliers. They transcribe data by hand ù or
 at best, scan barcodes into a disconnected spreadsheet ù before placing items in bins whose locations are tracked only in someone's memory.
1.2 The Core Bottleneck
The fundamental problem: unstructured, heterogeneous paper documents at the intake point cr
eate a data gap between the physical product and its digital record. This gap is where errors, delays, and compliance failures originate.
The specific failure modes are:
Manual transcription errors: A warehouse clerk transcribing a 12-digit batch number b
y hand introduces errors at rates estimated between 1û3%. In a pharma context, one wrong digit means a batch cannot be recalled accurately.
Document heterogeneity: Supplier A prints a structured invoice; Supplier B sends a handwritten chit; Supplier C prov
ides a label in a regional language. No single barcode scanner or template-based OCR system handles all three.
Physical placement latency: After data entry, the clerk must consult a paper bin map or a separate terminal to determine where to place the produ
ct. This adds 30û90 seconds per item in a busy warehouse.
Broken traceability chains: Because supplier, batch, and storage location are recorded in separate systems (or not at all), reconstructing a product'
s history for an audit or recall is time-consuming and error-prone.
Expiry mismanagement: Without automated expiry parsing and alerting at intake, near-expiry stock gets shelved in standard locations and discovered only during periodic audits ù often too l
ate.
1.3 Scale of the Problem
Consider a mid-size herbal products warehouse receiving 200 line items per day across 40 suppliers. Even at a conservative 2-minute manual processing time per item, that is over 6 person-hours per day spent purely on data entr
y ù work that generates no insight, only records. At a 2% error rate, four items per day carry incorrect metadata, compounding audit risk over time.
Larger pharmaceutical distributors face this problem at 10x the scale, with the added burden of regulatory frameworks such as Schedule H drug tracking, Cold Chain handling requirements, and Good Distribution Practice (GDP) mandates that require timestamped
, auditable intake records.

2. Existing Solutions and Their Limitations
2.1 Barcode and QR Code Scanning
The most widely deployed solution is GS1-standard barcode scanning at the receiving dock. Workers scan a barcode on each product and the WMS (Warehouse Management System) pulls th
e associated record from a pre-loaded product master.
Limitation ù 
Requires
 prior master data: The product must already exist in the system with its barcode pre-registered. New SKUs, ad-hoc supplier shipments, or products from non-standardised suppliers br
eak this flow entirely.
Limitation ù 
Does
 not parse document context: A barcode on a product label captures the product ID. It does not capture the batch number, expiry date, or supplier-specific lot information printed on the accompanying packing slip.
Li
mitation ù No handwriting support: Handwritten chits, informal delivery notes, and manually annotated invoices are completely invisible to barcode scanners.
2.2 Template-Based OCR Systems
Some mid-tier WMS platforms include OCR modules that can extract tex
t from invoices if the supplier uses a known, fixed template. The user maps field coordinates ("Batch No. is always at position x=210, y=340 on this supplier's invoice") and the system extracts accordingly.
Limitation ù 
Brittle
 to format variation: When a 
supplier changes their invoice template, the entire mapping breaks and requires manual reconfiguration by an IT administrator.
Limitation ù Zero tolerance for handwriting: Template OCR relies on consistent typography and layout. Handwritten annotations are
 treated as noise.
Limitation ù 
Does
 not understand context: If a field is partially obscured or the document is crumpled, the extraction fails silently ù returning an empty or wrong value without flagging the confidence issue.
2.3 Full ERP Integration (SAP, Oracle)
Enterprise pharmaceutical companies deploy end-to-end ERP systems with electronic data interchange (EDI) from suppliers, eliminating paper entirely. Suppliers submit structured XML or EDIFACT messages that map directl
y into the ERP.
Limitation ù Cost and supplier readiness: EDI integration costs tens of thousands of dollars per supplier connection. Small and mid-size herbal suppliers, particularly in emerging markets, cannot support EDI.
Limitation ù Physical placement is still manual: Even with perfect digital records, the ERP does not tell a worker which physical bin to place a product in, nor does it confirm placement.
Limitation ù No real-time physical feedback loop: The ERP records wh
at should happen; it does not verify what did happen at the bin level.
2.4 RFID-Based Systems
High-end distribution centres use RFID tags on pallets and cases, enabling passive scanning as goods move through a receiving gate.
Limitation ù Hardware cost and
 tagging requirement: RFID infrastructure (readers, portals, tags) is expensive. Each product must be pre-tagged, which requires supplier coordination.
Limitation ù Granularity gap: RFID tracks pallet or case movement, not individual unit batch records. Th
e document parsing problem at the item level remains unsolved.
Limitation ù No unstructured document handling: RFID does not address the packing slip, invoice, or handwritten label problem at all.

3. Our Unique Solution
3.1 Solution Overview
The Agentic Supply Chain Clerk is an end-to-end agentic pipeline that uses a multimodal Vision-Language Model to transform any physical intake document ù printed, handwritten, crumpled, or multilingual ù into validated structured data, then simultaneously 
updates a graph-based inventory record and directs 
IoT
 hardware to light the correct physical storage bin.
The term "agentic" is deliberate. Unlike a static OCR tool, the system reasons about what it sees. It applies contextual understanding to resolve am
biguous fields, flags low-confidence extractions for human review, and makes autonomous decisions about bin assignment based on product type, expiry date, and current stock levels ù all without requiring templates, pre-registration, or supplier coordinatio
n.
3.2 The Three-Layer Architecture Concept
Layer 1: Perception (Multimodal AI)
A high-resolution camera above the intake station captures the physical document. The image is passed to a Vision-Language Model (VLM) with a structured extraction prompt. The model returns a validated JSON object containing all 
parseable
 fields ù Batch No
., Expiry Date, Quantity, Supplier Name, Product Name, Unit of Measure ù along with a per-field confidence score. The model handles printed text, handwriting, mixed scripts, partial occlusion, and image noise without any template configuration.
Layer 2: In
telligence (Graph Database + Business Logic)
The 
FastAPI
 backend receives the JSON payload. It validates field formats (batch number patterns, date ranges, quantity bounds), then writes the record into a Neo4j graph database. The graph schema captures rela
tionships: a Supplier node connects to Batch nodes, which connect to Product nodes, which connect to 
StorageBin
 nodes. This graph structure enables queries that flat relational databases cannot answer efficiently ù for example, "find all batches from Suppl
ier X that expire within 30 days and are stored in Zone C." Simultaneously, the backend runs a bin assignment algorithm that selects the optimal bin based on product category, available capacity, and FEFO (First Expired, First Out) rules.
Layer 3: Action (
IoT
 Hardware)
The backend publishes a MQTT message containing the bin ID to an ESP32 microcontroller on the warehouse floor. The ESP32 drives an LED strip or individual LEDs mounted on the target bin, lighting it in a designated colour (green for standard 
placement, amber for near-expiry, red for quarantine). The worker simply places the product in the illuminated bin. A confirmation button or IR sensor on the bin sends an acknowledgement back to the ESP32, which publishes a confirmation MQTT message, closi
ng the feedback loop.
3.3 Key Differentiators
Zero template configuration: The VLM understands documents semantically, not by coordinate mapping. A new supplier requires no IT setup ù the first document from that supplier is processed correctly.
Handwritin
g as a first-class input: Handwritten batch numbers and expiry dates are extracted with the same pipeline as printed text, with confidence scoring to flag ambiguous characters.
Graph-native traceability: Neo4j captures the full provenance graph ù supplier,
 batch, product, bin, timestamp ù enabling instant recall impact assessment and audit trail generation.
Closed-loop physical confirmation: The system does not just assign a bin; it verifies that the worker placed the product in the correct bin via sensor a
cknowledgement, creating a physical audit trail.
Digital twin dashboard: A React-based warehouse twin visualises the real-time state of every bin ù occupancy, product, expiry, and supplier ù without requiring a worker to physically inspect the warehouse.

Step 1.1: Design the Extraction Prompt
The prompt is the most critical piece of engineering in the entire project. A poorly designed prompt produces inconsistent JSON structures that break downstream validation. The prompt
 must be stored as a versioned text file at backend/app/prompts/v1_extraction.txt. The prompt structure must include: (a) a role instruction establishing the model as a pharmaceutical document parser; (b) an explicit instruction to return ONLY valid JSON w
ith no surrounding prose; (c) a JSON schema definition with all expected fields, their types, and per-field confidence score; (d) handling instructions for ambiguous or illegible fields (return null with confidence 0.0, do not hallucinate); and (e) example
 output for a handwritten document.
Critical: The prompt must explicitly state 'If a field is illegible or absent, return null for the value and 0.0 for the confidence. Never invent or guess field values.' This prevents hallucination of batch numbers, whic
h is a patient safety issue.
